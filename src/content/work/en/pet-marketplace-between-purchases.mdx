## Context

A pet supplies retailer selling under two brands wanted more than an online shop. The product
that came out of it combines a catalogue and checkout with regular deliveries and a loyalty
programme, and around them pet profiles, walks recorded in the app, joint walks and playdates
with friends, local leaderboards, places for pets on a map, a community feed, health records with
reminders, consultations with specialists under a care subscription, pet shows, a barcode scanner
and a food advisor. It runs on the web and as a native app on both mobile platforms, built from one
codebase in three languages.

## Problem

Pet food is a repeat purchase and a price comparison. The customer buys the same bag every month and
checks where it is cheapest, large international marketplaces win that comparison on range and
price, and single-purpose pet apps own the time between purchases. A retailer that only sells the
bag gets chosen once a month, on price.

The obvious response is the opposite mistake: an app about the pet with a shop attached. An external
review of the user experience proposed exactly that, a carer's dashboard in place of the catalogue
as the main screen. It reads well, and it would have moved the place where the business earns its
money two taps away from where the customer lands. A product with sixty modules has a second, quieter
problem. Every feature competes for the same home screen, and without an agreed order of importance
each new screen settles that argument again on its own.

## Approach

The governing rule is marketplace-first, with the pet as the lens. The shop stays the main
experience. Activity, the map, the community, loyalty and health exist to give a reason to come back
between purchases, and every such surface has to lead back to commerce in at most two taps: a product
page, a regular delivery, the food advisor. The dashboard proposal was rejected on that basis, and
the useful parts of the same review were taken: plain words instead of internal terms, so customers
see regular deliveries rather than autoship, and pet signals moved into the shop instead of the shop
moving into the pet.

The pet profile is what makes the shop personal. From the pet's weight and the size of the pack, a
product page states how long a bag will last for that particular animal, and the dashboard shows how
many days of food are left and when the next delivery arrives. Suitability for the pet appears on the
product page and as a "for my pet" scope on listings, with the same allergen rules as the food
advisor. It is shown as a qualitative level with reasons, never as a percentage, because a figure
like "92% suitable" would be a claim about a product with nothing to defend it. How listings are
ordered is explained in plain words, including that no position is paid for. All of it works for
cats as well as dogs, a rule that sounds trivial until the map turns out to be titled "places for
dogs".

Two backends divide the work. A hosted commerce engine owns products, carts, orders and the back
office, and a domain backend owns everything specific to this product. The applications talk only to
the domain backend, which reaches the engine through an adapter. The engine was replaced once
already, in the first weeks, and that arrangement is what kept the change on the server.

Engagement is an economy, so it was designed and audited as one. Loyalty points, experience, badges
and streaks are separate currencies; experience can never be bought with points, and the only bridge
from activity to rewards is a single designed daily reward. An audit of the whole surface found the
defects such economies attract: reviews of products that were never bought were earning points, and a
walk claiming a kilometre in one second could take a local leaderboard crown. Points now require a
verified purchase, and an implausible pace still records the walk but cannot win. Showing who is
walking nearby blurs positions to a 500-metre grid, with a minimum crowd size, jitter and an expiry.

Health is where the product changed course most, and in the right direction. The first version
framed consultations as veterinary care and offered AI assessments of symptoms and skin changes, along
with suggestions for vaccinations, drug interactions and visit summaries. The people answering
consultations are specialists in behaviour, nutrition and training rather than vets, so the request
form stopped accepting health questions and the copy says "specialist" wherever it describes the
service. The health AI was then withdrawn altogether. In its place is a static page listing the
situations in which an owner should call a clinic open around the clock, with a search for one.
Medication warnings now come from rules rather than a model, and a prescription comes from the
owner's own vet and is verified by a person.

## Outcome

The platform is in production on the web and in the mobile stores. In November 2026 the brand's main
domain moved from its old hosted storefront to the platform. The hosted engine stays as the back
office, and indexed product addresses stay on the same host. Behind it are about sixty domain modules,
249 schema migrations and about 2,700 commits over eight months, delivered by a small team that also
runs the group's internal systems.

Some limits are set by others. The care subscription is paid by card outside the App Store, so the
iOS app does not show it, while members who joined on the web or on Android keep their benefits,
because the server grants them. The retailer's own strategy places deeper personalisation in a later
phase, and the pet-first changes so far are deliberately exposure of logic that already existed
rather than new mechanics.

## What it taught me

In a product with many features, the most consequential design decision is the order of importance,
written down so that each screen does not have to decide it again. "Marketplace-first, the pet as the
lens" settled dozens of arguments, including the decision to turn down a well-argued review.

The second lesson concerns what to remove. The health AI worked, in the sense that it produced
plausible answers, and removing it was the right product decision, because nobody behind the service
was in a position to answer for those answers. The boundary of a care product is drawn by the
competence of the people standing behind its advice, not by what a model can generate.
