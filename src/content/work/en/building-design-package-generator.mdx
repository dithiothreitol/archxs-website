## Context

A manufacturing group was preparing several capital projects at once: a grain distillery on an
existing plant site, two rendering halls and a small agricultural biogas plant. Each of them needs a
building permit design, meaning a site development plan, an architectural and construction design, a
technical design, the formal path of decisions and agreements, and the calculations behind all of it.
In Poland the form of that documentation is set by regulation, and a licensed designer signs it and
carries the liability.

## Problem

Design offices bill this work by volume, and most of the volume is not design. It is reading a local
zoning plan, establishing which decisions are needed and in what order, carrying room programmes into
drawings, checking distances between buildings against fire regulations, and assembling the same facts
into a dozen documents that have to agree with one another. When one parameter changes, that agreement
is maintained by hand, and that is where it breaks.

The obvious AI answer, a model that drafts the documentation, fails on the property that matters most.
A design package is read by people looking for the paragraph each statement rests on. A model that cites
building regulations from memory produces paragraph numbers that look right, and a design volume with
one invented citation is worse than a blank page, because the reviewer can no longer trust the rest of
it.

## Approach

I built the generator around three rules that the code enforces rather than the prompt requests. One
source of truth: every drawing, schedule and document is produced from one project file and a knowledge
base, outputs are never edited by hand, and changing a parameter rebuilds the whole package. Every number
has a source: a process parameter, a distance or a requirement carries a reference to a paragraph of
law, to literature or to a manufacturer's datasheet, or it is marked as an assumption and lands in a
register on which the designer has to decide. Regulations come only from files: legal acts are fetched
from the official legislation API with the date and address, each file names the consolidated text it
comes from and the amendments published after it, and the tests find every rule's quoted fragment
verbatim in the text of the act. Values from regulation tables live as data with their source, never as
numbers in code.

The core knows nothing about a particular kind of building. It handles the plot and the zoning plan, the
massing and the IFC model, fire zones, formal triggers, documents and the completeness checklist. Plant
knowledge lives in domain modules: a distillery with its mass and energy balance, column sizing, P&ID and
explosion zones; a biogas plant with its substrate balance, digester sizing and gas storage matched to
the operating windows in the grid connection agreement. A condition on the plant type inside the core is
treated as a design error. Cross-consistency is a build gate: a room or equipment tag that appears in a
schedule but not in the P&ID or the IFC model stops the build.

Missing data is never guessed. It goes to a list of open items stating which document it blocks and who
has to supply it. Paid standards are not reproduced either; a result that needs one is marked as such and
left to the designer.

Around the generator sits a web application that takes an employee through seven stages, with checks
that come with ready fixes and a preview of their effect before anything is saved. Its copilot takes
numbers from the generator, regulations from the legal files and procedures from versioned skills, and
proposes changes as cards to approve. The same skills, reviewer subagents and hooks ship as a Claude Code
plugin, with evaluation cases such as citing the law without relying on memory.

Before handing anything over I ran an audit of the package and of the generator itself: five independent
reviews, each confirming its findings in the code, the legal text or the output file. It found what a
designer would have found, among others a module building that failed the zoning check while still
feeding the package summary, a utilities balance check that could never fail, and fire road and distance
rules applied too narrowly. The fixes went in before the handover, not after it.

## Outcome

The distillery pilot reached the designers eleven days after the first commit, as a complete package:
drawings held to the standard of an approved permit design used as the reference, an IFC model with a 3D
preview, technical descriptions and a verification report. It stands at 173 requirements met, 62 gaps and
no errors, with 41 open items, every one of them waiting on someone outside the project, such as the grid
operator, the road authority or a geotechnical survey. The rendering halls and the biogas plant run
through the same core, and the biogas plant's technical data and schedule of works are meant to feed the
loan documentation the group prepares for its bank.

The limits are part of the design. The generator does not sign anything and does not replace the
designer's judgement; it moves their work from assembling to checking. It cannot apply a standard it does
not hold, and its legal knowledge is exactly as current as the last fetch.

## What it taught me

The useful boundary for AI in a regulated discipline is not the model's accuracy but the source of each
statement. Once every number and every citation has to point at a file, most of what a model would be
tempted to invent has nowhere to go, and what remains is visible as an assumption.

The second lesson is about auditing your own output before someone else does. The reviews found errors
the tests had not, because the tests checked what I had thought of, while the audit read the package the
way its first reader would.
