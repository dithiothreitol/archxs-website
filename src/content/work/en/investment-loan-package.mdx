## Context

The group's internal assistant, a platform of AI tools used across the company, gained a new mode for
one recurring job: preparing an investment loan application for a bank. The reference case was a small
agricultural biogas plant, and the package the bank expects is familiar to anyone who has financed a
project: a financial model, a business plan, a schedule of works and spending, the general contractor's
offer and contract, and a set of source documents from permits to financial statements.

## Problem

The manual package for the reference case had been prepared carefully, and it still contradicted
itself. The business plan stated that the minimum debt service cover ratio met bank standards, while the
model behind it gave 1.10 in the first year of repayment, below the threshold the plan itself quoted. The
model's balance sheet failed to balance in one year by more than three million złoty, several columns
showed broken references, formulas pointed at an external file, the loan was drawn in a single sum rather
than in tranches following the schedule, and the supporting company's balance sheet was described with
the wrong date.

None of these were exotic mistakes. They are what happens when five documents are maintained by hand
around one set of numbers. Then the bank asked for ten months of construction instead of seven. That one
change required edits in four documents, and with the grace period left as it was, the cover ratio in the
first year of repayment fell below one. Nobody would have noticed until the bank did.

## Approach

The governing rule is one case, one set of assumptions, one calculation engine, every document. No number
in any document is typed by hand or written by a language model.

Assumptions start from sources. The user uploads whatever the client sent, scanned permits included, and
the model extracts facts with a verbatim quotation and a page number. Each fact is accepted or rejected
next to the original with the quotation highlighted, and when two documents disagree, the user has to
choose between them. An accepted value enters the assumptions with its provenance, so every field can
open the document it came from at the right page. Company data comes from the court register, the
statistics office and the VAT white list rather than from anyone's memory.

The engine computes capital expenditure month by month, loan tranches, interest capitalised during
construction, repayments, depreciation, the income statement, cash flow and balance sheet, cover ratios,
IRR and NPV, and a set of scenarios. A cockpit of key ratios stays on screen, and every change shows its
consequence before it is saved: the minimum cover ratio moving from one value to another, and the list of
documents that would become stale.

Problems are presented as tasks with a fix rather than as red messages. Twenty-three rules check the case,
from a first repayment falling before revenue starts, through a cash gap in a given month, to construction
running outside the window set by the grid connection agreement. Most come with a prepared correction,
such as choosing the grace period or adding an owner's loan in the month of the gap, shown as a preview
and applied only on explicit confirmation. A separate review has Claude read the case as a bank credit
analyst looking for reasons to refuse, returning red flags, questions and strengths, and its findings
feed the next version of the business plan.

The documents follow from the same state. The model is generated as a spreadsheet with live formulas, the
schedule as a spreadsheet and a document, and the business plan is written by Claude chapter by chapter
while the engine inserts every number. The contractor's contract is generated as a draft marked on every
page as requiring legal review and goes on to the group's contract review tool. The bank checklist
assembles the latest current versions with the source files and records exactly what was sent.

Each case has a copilot that knows its assumptions, results and unsaved changes. It answers a question
such as "how do we reach a cover ratio of 1.2?" by running the engine's solvers for grace period, loan
share or number of instalments, never by estimating. It proposes and never saves, and it is not allowed
to adjust prices, production or costs to improve a ratio, because those are facts from documents.

## Outcome

The mode was built in five coding sessions, each closed by a code review, and is covered by around 160
backend tests. On the reference case the bank's request is now a single change. Extending construction to
ten months moves the start of revenue and raises two blocking findings; the grace period fix moves the
first instalment and lifts the minimum cover ratio from 0.87 to 1.25, leaving one finding, a short cash
gap in a single month, with a fix of its own.

The tool states what it does not do. It does not verify the contract legally, does not model VAT
financing, does not compute LLCR or dividends, and does not assess creditworthiness on the bank's behalf.
It shows the risks a bank will ask about, and the decision stays with the bank.

## What it taught me

A loan application is an argument made with numbers, and its weakest point is rarely the arithmetic. It
is the distance between the number in the model and the sentence in the plan. Generating both from one
state removes that distance instead of checking it after the fact.

The second lesson concerns where to put the model. Claude earns its place here in exactly three roles:
reading scans, writing prose and arguing as a sceptical analyst. Each of them is bounded by an engine
that owns the numbers, and the copilot's most important rule is the one that forbids it from making the
case look better than the documents allow.
